# The Forty Minutes (draft 2)

The lens was cleaned on the first dry morning of the month, and the first dry morning that March was a Tuesday. Mrs. Rook sat me on the step stool inside the lamp with a bowl of warm water, a jar of whiting and the soft leather, and she sat below on the gallery bench with the almanac open on her knee.

"Ring by ring," she said. "Top down. You never go back up over a ring you have done."

The glass was cut in rings like the inside of a shell, and every ring held a gray bloom of soot from the winter. I wet the leather and dipped it in the whiting and worked the first ring the way the Board's inspector had shown me at the depot, until the bloom came off on the cloth. Then the next. By the fourth my arms ached. Down in the room the wind kept on at the shutters.

Mrs. Rook read the tides aloud while I worked. She did it every morning, as other people say grace.

"Wednesday. High water, nine and twelve. And forty." She turned the page with her thumb. "Thursday. High water, nine and fifty-one. And forty."

"And forty what?" I said.

"Read them yourself, child. I cannot see the print any more." Then, "Mind your ring."

I minded it. The almanac was printed in Saltby for the harbor there. Dell brought a new one every January with the oil, and it was the only book in the house besides the log and the Bible. I had read it through twice that winter for want of anything else, and there was no "and forty" anywhere in it.

"You said it Monday too," I said.

"Then I said it Monday." She wiped her hands on her apron, though she had not touched anything. "Friday. Ten and thirty. And forty."

My grandmother had done the same at the end. She had tied one word to the end of every sentence like a knot at the end of a thread, and the parish had come for her in the spring with the cart. I did not want the cart coming for Mrs. Rook. So I made up my mind to tell Dell about it, so that he could tell the parish. I took the leather to the last ring before the middle. The whiting dried white in the cuts of the glass, and when I rubbed it off, the ring threw the window back at me upside down, small and bright, with Mrs. Rook in it, bent over the almanac. Then I changed the water and started on the bull's-eye, which was the hardest, because it showed every smear.

When the sun was over the chimney she sent me down the causeway to the third post to look at the weed. "Look and come back," she said. "Do not stand about."

The causeway was bare for about four hours at each low water, and you learned to walk it fast anyway. The stones were green to the second post and brown after it. The weed on the third post was still wet a hand's width above the mud, which meant the water had only just gone off it. I stood and looked at it longer than she would have liked. By the almanac, low water had been an hour ago.

I rubbed the whiting off my thumb onto my skirt and walked back slowly. At the second post a man from Saltby I had never seen before was digging bait, and he told me the holm's water always ran forty minutes behind the almanac. I did not tell her that either.
