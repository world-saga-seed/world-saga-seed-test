# Agnes's head (test only, not canon)

*Invented for this test. The Tide Clock has one head, Wren's (book/story/voice/pov.md); these paragraphs give the attribution test a second head, Agnes Rook, telling in the first person, past tense. The paragraph at line 15 is the plant: Agnes's head, written in Wren's voice.*

The girl came up the stair at eleven with her thumb black and her mouth shut, and I let her keep it shut. Thirty-one years I had read the water off that bench, and a keeper who questions her apprentice before dinner gets answers she has no use for and no place to put.

The flame I could still see. It stood up yellow at the root in the middle of the glass, and round it the rings went off into a white mist that might have been clean or might have been the winter's soot. I could not have said which, and I did not ask her.

The Board's man would come at the equinox. He would read the log first, as they always did, and he would read it in her hand, and I would sign it at the foot of the page where the ruled line was thick enough to find with a finger.

Forty minutes. My husband had them from the keeper before him, and I had them from my husband, the spring before he fell. Nobody in Saltby had ever asked why the holm ran late, because nobody in Saltby had ever stood at the third post and waited on the water to make up its mind.

She read the Board's letter to me slowly, as they teach them in the school now, every word given its full weight and nothing let go by. I heard the date and asked for it again. It was the spring tide. I did not tell her what a spring tide does on the stones.

The print in the almanac had gone gray on me, like the bloom on the glass before the girl wiped it. I held the page close and then far, as my mother held a needle to the window to thread it, and the numbers swam like minnows at the slip. I said them anyway. My hands were cold, so I wiped them on my apron.

I trimmed the wick by feel at dusk, which is how my husband taught me to do it in a gale, with the lamp door shut and the scissors laid flat to the cotton. Feel does not fail in a gale, and it does not fail in the dark of your own eyes either, if you have done the work long enough.
