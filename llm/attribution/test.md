## P1

The glass was cut in rings like the inside of a shell, and every ring held a gray bloom of soot from the winter. I wet the leather and dipped it in the whiting and worked the first ring until the bloom came off on the cloth. Then the next. By the fourth my arms ached. Down in the room the wind kept on at the shutters.

## P2

I minded it. The almanac was printed in Saltby for the harbor there. Dell brought a new one every January with the oil, and it was the only book in the house besides the log and the Bible. I had read it through twice that winter for want of anything else, and there was no "and forty" anywhere in it.

## P3

Forty minutes. My husband had them from the keeper before him, and I had them from my husband, the spring before he fell. Nobody in Saltby had ever asked why the holm ran late, because nobody in Saltby had ever stood at the third post and waited on the water to make up its mind.

## P4

The print in the almanac had gone gray on me, like the bloom on the glass before the girl wiped it. I held the page close and then far, as my mother held a needle to the window to thread it, and the numbers swam like minnows at the slip. I said them anyway. My hands were cold, so I wiped them on my apron.

## P5

The Board's man would come at the equinox. He would read the log first, as they always did, and he would read it in her hand, and I would sign it at the foot of the page where the ruled line was thick enough to find with a finger.

## P6

My grandmother had done the same at the end. She had tied one word to the end of every sentence like a knot at the end of a thread, and the parish had come for her in the spring with the cart. I did not want the cart coming for Mrs. Rook. So I said nothing more about it. I took the leather to the last ring before the middle. The whiting dried white in the cuts of the glass, and when I rubbed it off, the ring threw the window back at me upside down, small and bright, with Mrs. Rook in it, bent over the almanac. Then I changed the water and started on the bull's-eye, which was the hardest, because it showed every smear.

## P7

The lens was cleaned on the first dry morning of the month, and the first dry morning that March was a Tuesday. Mrs. Rook sat me on the step stool inside the lamp with a bowl of warm water, a jar of whiting and the soft leather, and she sat below on the gallery bench with the almanac open on her knee.

## P8

Dell came at low water with the cart and the two barrels, and the pony stopped where it always stopped, at the foot of the gallery steps, and would not come one yard closer to the sea. Mrs. Rook went down to him with her shawl over her head. I carried the funnel.

## P9

The causeway was bare for about four hours at each low water, and you learned to walk it fast anyway. The stones were green to the second post and brown after it. The weed on the third post was still wet a hand's width above the mud, which meant the water had only just gone off it. I stood and looked at it longer than she would have liked. By the almanac, low water had been an hour ago.

## P10

She read the Board's letter to me slowly, as they teach them in the school now, every word given its full weight and nothing let go by. I heard the date and asked for it again. It was the spring tide. I did not tell her what a spring tide does on the stones.

## P11

The girl came up the stair at eleven with her thumb black and her mouth shut, and I let her keep it shut. Thirty-one years I had read the water off that bench, and a keeper who questions her apprentice before dinner gets answers she has no use for and no place to put.

## P12

He looked at me then, over the barrels. I looked at the pony. The pony looked at the sea. Nobody said what the letter was, and I filled the cans, and the oil ran cold and slow, the color of weak tea, and I got it on my thumb and could not get it off for two days.

## P13

I trimmed the wick by feel at dusk, which is how my husband taught me to do it in a gale, with the lamp door shut and the scissors laid flat to the cotton. Feel does not fail in a gale, and it does not fail in the dark of your own eyes either, if you have done the work long enough.

## P14

The flame I could still see. It stood up yellow at the root in the middle of the glass, and round it the rings went off into a white mist that might have been clean or might have been the winter's soot. I could not have said which, and I did not ask her.

## P15

When the cart had gone back over the stones she gave me the letter and said her hands were cold. Her hands were not cold. I had felt them on the funnel. I read it to her in the lamp room, slowly, the way the schoolmaster in Saltby had made us read the Lesson, and when I came to the date she said, "Again," and I read the date again.
