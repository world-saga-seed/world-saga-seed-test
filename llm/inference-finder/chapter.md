# The Forty Minutes (draft 2)

The lens was cleaned on the first dry morning of the month, and the first dry morning that March was a Tuesday. Mrs. Rook sat me on the step stool inside the lamp with a bowl of warm water, a jar of whiting and the soft leather, and she sat below on the gallery bench with the almanac open on her knee.

"Ring by ring," she said. "Top down. You never go back up over a ring you have done."

The glass was cut in rings like the inside of a shell, and every ring held a gray bloom of soot from the winter. I wet the leather and dipped it in the whiting and worked the first ring until the bloom came off on the cloth. Then the next. By the fourth my arms ached. Down in the room the wind kept on at the shutters.

Mrs. Rook read the tides aloud while I worked. She did it every morning, as other people say grace.

"Wednesday. High water, nine and twelve. And forty." She turned the page with her thumb. "Thursday. High water, nine and fifty-one. And forty."

"And forty what?" I said.

"Mind your ring," she said, sharp, and she shut the almanac on her thumb.

She was cross with me for asking, so I minded it. The almanac was printed in Saltby for the harbor there. Dell brought a new one every January with the oil, and it was the only book in the house besides the log and the Bible. I had read it through twice that winter for want of anything else, and there was no "and forty" anywhere in it.

"You said it Monday too," I said.

"Then I said it Monday." She wiped her hands on her apron, though she had not touched anything. "Friday. Ten and thirty. And forty."

My grandmother had done the same at the end. She had tied one word to the end of every sentence like a knot at the end of a thread, and the parish had come for her in the spring with the cart. I did not want the cart coming for Mrs. Rook. Mrs. Rook hated reading the almanac out, and she was ashamed of it. So I said nothing more about it.

I took the leather to the last ring before the middle. The whiting dried white in the cuts of the glass, and when I rubbed it off, the ring threw the window back at me upside down, small and bright, with Mrs. Rook in it, bent over the almanac. Then I changed the water and started on the bull's-eye, which was the hardest, because it showed every smear.

When the sun was over the chimney she sent me down the causeway to the third post to look at the weed. "Look and come back," she said. "Do not stand about."

The causeway was bare for about four hours at each low water, and you learned to walk it fast anyway. The stones were green to the second post and brown after it. The weed on the third post was still wet a hand's width above the mud, which meant the water had only just gone off it. I stood and looked at it longer than she would have liked. By the almanac, low water had been an hour ago.

I rubbed the whiting off my thumb onto my skirt and walked back slowly, and I did not tell her that either.

The lamp room smelled of whiting and oil when I came up. Mrs. Rook had put the almanac back on the shelf beside the log and the Board's Instructions, and she was trimming the wick with the long scissors.

I emptied the bowl over the rail and set the stool back under the lamp.

She had sent me to the post to be rid of my questions, and she did not want to know what the weed said.

We ate at noon at the table under the stair, bread and the end of the cheese. She cut the bread against her chest with the knife coming toward her, as my grandmother used to, and gave me the heel.

"Eat," she said. "The brass will not do itself."

After we ate I scoured the oil cans by the door, for Dell to take back to Saltby with the cart. Dell had fished out of Saltby before the Board gave him the oil, and he had lost a brother on the sands below the holm.

The afternoon went on the brass. I did the gallery rail and the door of the lamp, and the handles of the oil store, and my thumb went black and stayed black.

Mrs. Rook stood at the gallery rail with her shawl over her head and watched the water come back over the causeway. It came in across the sand in long flat sheets, faster than a person walks.

She was thinking of her husband, as she always did when the tide turned.

I worked the polish into the hinge of the lamp door until it shone, and then I did it again, so as not to stand beside her.

The wind dropped at four. The sheets of water met over the stones, and the first post went under, and then the second. The gulls came in off the sand to the rocks under the gallery.

The gallery rail was green at every joint, because the Board had sent cheap brass the year the gallery was built.

At dusk she lit the lamp herself, and I wrote the time in the log, because my hand was better.
